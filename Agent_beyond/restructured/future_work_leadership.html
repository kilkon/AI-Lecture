Future of Work

The Future of Work: Skills, Labor, and Managed Agent Systems

Recent debate is no longer a simple battle between "AI will destroy jobs" and "AI will create jobs." The harder question is how work, authority, skills, and bargaining power are reorganized when agents become everyday infrastructure.

Displacement concern

One side of the debate emphasizes labor-market disruption, especially in cognitive and clerical work.

  • IMF (January 14, 2024): AI may affect about 40% of jobs worldwide and around 60% in advanced economies.
  • IMF working paper (September 13, 2024): U.S. regions with higher AI adoption saw stronger employment decline in manufacturing and low-skill services.
  • Many fear a loss of bargaining power, routine white-collar roles, and middle-skill career ladders.

Augmentation argument

The competing view is that exposure does not automatically mean full automation.

  • ILO (May 20, 2025): updated occupational exposure work still suggests augmentation is more common than full replacement.
  • WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025: 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced by 2030, for a net gain of 78 million jobs.
  • The central challenge becomes transition quality, not only aggregate job totals.

Emerging organizational reality

What is actually arriving inside organizations is a managed human-agent system.

  • Anthropic Economic Index (updated March 24, 2026) tracks AI use inside real tasks, not only theory.
  • Jobs are being redesigned around orchestration, verification, escalation, and tool use.
  • Some agents may coordinate other agents, creating a bounded form of operational leadership.

Skills that become more valuable

Analytical judgment Verification and audit Workflow design Data stewardship Cross-functional collaboration Leadership and escalation

The premium shifts from doing every subtask manually to supervising systems that combine people, agents, and tools.

Implication for universities and employers

Human leadership and agent leadership can coexist, but only if institutions deliberately redesign jobs, authority, and reskilling pathways.

That means preparing graduates and employees not only to use AI, but to supervise, orchestrate, govern, and challenge it when needed.
